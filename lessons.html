Grammar القواعد

Grammar basics, sentence structure, and writing practice.

1 Personal Pronouns الضمائر

Arabic pronouns change depending on who is speaking and whether the subject is one person, two, or many. Unlike English, Arabic also has a special "dual" form for exactly two people or things, and separate masculine/feminine forms for "you" and "they" — something English doesn't have at all.

EnglishArabicPronunciation
Iأَنَاana
You (m.)أَنْتَanta
You (f.)أَنْتِanti
Heهُوَhuwa
Sheهِيَhiya
Weنَحْنُnaḥnu
Theyهُمْhum
Ahmed says:

A quick trick — "أَنْتَ" (you, masculine) and "أَنْتِ" (you, feminine) sound almost the same, just listen for that final "a" vs "i" sound!

2 Basic Sentence Structure تركيب الجملة

Arabic has two main sentence types. A verbal sentence starts with a verb; a nominal sentence starts with a noun or pronoun and often needs no verb "to be" in the present tense.

Nominal example: البَيْتُ كَبِيرٌ — literally "the-house big" — meaning "The house is big."

Verbal example: يَذْهَبُ الوَلَدُ إِلَى المَدْرَسَةِ — literally "goes the-boy to the-school" — meaning "The boy goes to school." Notice the verb comes first.

Word order is flexible in Arabic compared to English — moving the subject before the verb (يَذْهَبُ → الوَلَدُ يَذْهَبُ) is still correct, but shifts the emphasis onto who is doing the action rather than the action itself.

3 Present-Tense Verb Basics الفعل المضارع

Present-tense verbs change their prefix and sometimes suffix depending on the subject. Here is the verb "to write" — يَكْتُبُ (yaktubu):

SubjectArabicMeaning
أَنَا (I)أَكْتُبُI write
أَنْتَ (you, m.)تَكْتُبُyou write
هُوَ (he)يَكْتُبُhe writes
هِيَ (she)تَكْتُبُshe writes
نَحْنُ (we)نَكْتُبُwe write

Notice the pattern: most forms share the root letters ك ت ب (k-t-b, "write"), with a different prefix for each subject. This three-letter root system is the backbone of almost all Arabic vocabulary — once you recognize a root, you can often guess the meaning of related words.

4 Definite Article "Al-" أل التعريف

Adding الـ ("al-") to the start of a noun makes it definite, similar to "the" in English.

كِتَاب (kitab, "a book") → الكِتَاب (al-kitab, "the book")

There's no separate word for "a/an" in Arabic — a noun without الـ is automatically understood as indefinite. So كِتَاب simply means "a book" on its own, no extra word needed.

5 The Feminine Marker تاء التأنيث

Many feminine nouns and adjectives end in a special letter shape called tāʾ marbūṭa (ة), pronounced like a soft "a" at the end of a word (or "at" when followed by another word in certain grammatical constructions).

مُعَلِّم (muʿallim, "male teacher") → مُعَلِّمَة (muʿallima, "female teacher")

طَالِب (ṭālib, "male student") → طَالِبَة (ṭāliba, "female student")

Maryam says:

Spotting a ة at the end of a word is one of the fastest ways to guess it's feminine — try it next time you're reading!

6 Possession: The Iḍāfa Construction الإضافة

Arabic shows possession by simply placing two nouns next to each other — no separate word for "of" is needed. The first noun drops its الـ, and the second noun carries the "the".

بَيْتُ الطَّالِبِ — literally "house (of) the-student" — meaning "the student's house."

كِتَابُ المُعَلِّمَةِ — literally "book (of) the-teacher(f.)" — meaning "the teacher's book."
